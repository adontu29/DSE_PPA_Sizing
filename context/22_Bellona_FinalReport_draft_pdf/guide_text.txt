The Design Synthesis Excercise
The scope and objectives
The Design Synthesis Exercise (AE3-200) is scheduled in the 2nd part of the 2nd semester of the academic year (with a “resit” in the 2nd part of the first semester) and constitutes the concluding part of the Bachelor’s study program (BSc). The objectives of the Design Synthesis Exercise are for the students to enhance their skills in:
Designing
Applying knowledge
Communicating (discussion, presentation, report)
Working as a team
Sustainable development

Because this design exercise is part of the undergraduate program it is not the aim to attain a flawless final result, able to compete with contemporary standards in industrial practice. Although the student, together with the tutors, will have the natural drive to create "the best design", it is expected that design choices will be made which will not lead to the optimal result. These choices can only be partially iterated within the limited timeframe of the exercise. Such an iterative process is an invaluable element in obtaining design experience.

The technical result will therefore be just one of the aspects that will be considered in the grading. Performing the design process as a team is an essential part of the exercise, because it reflects most industrial design approaches. It puts the students in the position that enables them to fully devote their personal skill as part of a greater organisation. 

For the grading the way in which the design process is developed and executed is therefore as important as the technical result itself:
is the project executed as an integrated project rather than a set of independent activities?
does the group work as a team rather than a number of individuals?

During the exercise the students will come across fields of knowledge and supervisors from various chairs within the faculty. The exercise offers students the opportunity to orient themselves on the different main subjects and gain insight into his or her specific capabilities and fields of interest. Thus the exercise provides additional support to choosing the M.Sc program.

The Assignment
Context
High-altitude balloons are widely used for meteorology, environmental sensing, and commercial experiments. Occasionally, such balloons drift into restricted or populated airspace (e.g. near airports), creating a need for safe, controlled recovery without damage to people, infrastructure, operations, or the environment.

The goal is to develop a drone-based solution that can detect, track, and interact with an uncooperative balloon in a controlled and non-destructive manner. The emphasis is on gentle intervention techniques—such as guided descent or gradual deflation—rather than forceful or hazardous approaches. Safety, reliability, and sustainability are central design drivers throughout the project.





Project Objective and Top-Level Requirements
OBJECTIVE:
Design and prototype a small unmanned aerial system (UAS) capable of locating, intercepting, and safely disabling or recovering a free-floating balloon in a controlled manner.


REQUIREMENTS:
Performance
The balloon is uncooperative (no onboard control, unknown drift)
The system shall be capable of detecting and tracking a free-floating balloon with a diameter of 2–6 m at altitudes up to 300 m AGL.
The UAS shall be able to intercept the balloon within 10 minutes of visual or sensor acquisition under nominal wind conditions (≤ 8 m/s).
Safety and reliability
Assume operation in civilian airspace with strict safety requirements.
All interaction mechanisms shall be non-explosive, non-pyrotechnic, non-destructive and pose no cutting hazard to people on the ground. The solution must avoid falling debris larger than 50g or uncontrolled descent.
The system shall include redundant positioning and attitude estimation (e.g., GNSS + visual or inertial backup).
The system shall tolerate single-point failures in sensors or actuators without catastrophic outcomes.
Pre-flight risk assessment and checklists are mandatory.
Sustainability
The system shall be designed to minimise material waste, favouring reusable or recyclable components.
Energy storage shall be electric only (no combustion engines).
The balloon recovery method shall aim to prevent dispersion of debris or cargo and allow retrieval and proper disposal where feasible.
Noise levels during operation should be kept below 65 dB at 50 m.
Life-cycle considerations (manufacturing, operation, disposal) must be discussed in the final report.
Cost
The total cost of the unmanned aerial system, including airframe, propulsion, onboard computer, sensors, communication hardware, and interaction mechanisms, shall not exceed €5,000. This cost represents a one-time acquisition cost and excludes ground control equipment.
Design choices should favour modularity and reusability to minimize replacement costs following routine operation or minor failures.
The estimated cost per deployment shall not exceed €500. Per-deployment cost may include energy consumption (battery charging or replacement), consumable components (e.g., tethers, puncture elements, recovery bags), routine wear and maintenance attributable to a single mission.
Designs that rely on single-use or disposable components must explicitly justify their necessity and cost impact.
Validation Requirements
At least two failure scenarios must be demonstrated or analysed (e.g. lost target, partial mechanism failure).

Project outline
The first action to be taken by the students is that the group should organise itself. Responsibilities should become clear within the group and the required disciplines in the project should be assessed and a project plan should be written.

Next, the team should start with short literature surveys to get acquainted with the most important elements of the project. These include aircraft design, aerodynamic control, propeller propulsion, composite materials, and structures, and rotor acoustics as well as recent news on similar incidents. In addition, the team should investigate current similar solutions if they find any and to identify the full characteristics of such balloons (total weight, payload, size, speed, gas etc). The findings and conclusions of this phase are reported in the baseline review.

The team will be required to take a series of interdependent system-level design decisions. First, the choice of aerial platform and propulsion concept (e.g. multirotor, hybrid VTOL, fixed wing with recovery mechanism) will significantly influence performance, endurance, controllability near the target, and operational cost. Second, the team must define the sensing and tracking approach (e.g. vision-based, GNSS-assisted, radar-assisted), which directly affects robustness, autonomy level, and system complexity. Third, a decision must be made regarding the interaction strategy with the balloon, such as tethered recovery, controlled deflation, or guided descent, each with distinct implications for safety, reliability, per-deployment cost, and environmental impact.

These choices are strongly coupled and involve trade-offs across multiple engineering domains. The team is expected to develop and evaluate several alternative system concepts (approximately four) that reflect different combinations of these design decisions. Each concept should be analysed in sufficient technical detail to enable a fair comparison in terms of feasibility, safety, cost (both UAS acquisition and per-deployment), and compliance with the project requirements.

In the last phase of the project, the selected concept will be designed in full detail. All subsystem risks will be investigated, contingencies considered, and a final report will contain the details of the design according to the delivery table above. The team will present the final design during the final review.

The Deliverable Items
The project shall realise the following deliverable items:

Project Plan which shall include items like an Organisational Breakdown Structure (organogram), a Work Breakdown Structure (WBS), a schedule for the OS design project with project phasing and the planning for the delivery of the items presented in a bar chart, a project logic diagram (activity flow), and an approach with respect to sustainable development.

Mid-Term Technical Design Report has to include design concepts, candidate techniques, critical aspects (design options tree), the trade method, rationale and organisation, including criteria and weight factors, the design justifications, a resource allocation (budget and performance predictions), a conceptual configuration definition and a technical risk assessment/ risk map.

Final Technical Design Report has to include all topics mentioned for the Final Technical Design, a summary of most important findings of the Mid-Term Report, and an Executive Summary in English.

References in deliverable reports
Used references during the DSE and in the deliverable reports should have a sufficient level and quality. The use of Wikipedia should be limited, and if information is used from internet it would be wise to verify this information (if possible, of course). A list of references is an essential part of each (technical) report, and the rules in Appendix D have to be followed. Pay special attention to the inclusion of websites. Typical examples are product and/or project specifications, or general Wikipedia references (to be avoided!). Include these as footnote in the text (not in the reference list), e.g., URL http://www.nasa.gov/centers/ames/research/msl_heatshield.html [cited 13 April 1998].

In general: no URLs should be included in the reference list; websites in footnotes, and date of last access should be mentioned.

Absence Regulation
The following rules regarding absence hold:

The group works for 8 hours a day, excluding breaks. In consultation with the Teaching Assistants, the group can agree on working hours and breaks. It is not allowed to start later than 9:00 or to end the day earlier than 17:30. 
Students are allowed to miss a maximum of 2 days for the entirety of the exercise. This is for unforeseen as well as foreseen circumstances.
Any missing time (either by being late or leaving at any time, irrespective its duration) during DSE will result in being registered as a missed day. Absence, late arrival and/or early departure are recorded. 
Absence during obligatory key events of the DSE (e.g. Baseline, Mid-term and Final Review, DSE symposium, grading, etc.) is not allowed under any condition. 

As soon as a student has been absent on more than two dates, no matter its cause or duration, he or she will be expelled from the exercise for the running period. Experience has shown that students spend all their time towards the design project and have insufficient time left to make a serious preparation for an exam. Still, if a student wants to take an exam, the following conditions apply:

The DSE project planning does not accommodate exams.
The DSE absence regulations and project demands prevail: absence to prepare or take an exam has to be compliant with these regulations.

Every absence has to be reported to the principal tutor and the Teaching Assistants. Every foreseen leave of absence has to be discussed with the principal tutor during the first week of the exercise. If for health reasons unexpected absence occurs, please consult with Joris Melkert. 

Only after successful completion a total of 15 credits will be awarded.

Grading
Grading a project group is a difficult matter but should be practical at the same time. Recognition must be given for both the achievement of the group as a whole as well as the individual contribution to the group result. The faculty’s Board of Examination requires grading each student individually. The grade should be a good reflection of how the student evolved during the design project. Hence the input to grading has to be collected at several moments. Suitable grading moments include amongst others the reviews and after reading the final report. The final grade for a student will be determined in a meeting between the principal tutor and the project coaches after reading the final report. An OSCC member facilitates this meeting to ensure uniform application of standards across all groups.

The principal tutor will set a date for a group/student meeting, to communicate the achievement in individual feedback sessions to the students (together with the project coaches), and transmit the grades to the OSCC.

The System Engineering aspects of the DSE are checked by external experts. To assist in this, each group has to co-evaluate the deliverables on PM/SE aspects of two other groups. The feedback provided by the groups will also be taken into account in the overall grading. The results are communicated with the group, tutor and coaches.

There are two official grading moments, i.e., one shortly after the midterm review and one shortly after the final review. For each of these gradings both a group grade and an individual grade will be established. The final individual grade is a weighted average of those four grades, as indicated in the figure below.

Project Management / Systems Engineering
To guarantee success and efficiency in a complex aerospace design project, project management and system engineering is essential. The DSE is the only project where you can apply theory, as you have been taught in AE3211-I - Systems Engineering & Aerospace Design, and develop the relevant skills in a practical environment. When applied properly, PM/SE will save you quite some time in design work and it will avoid pitfalls that will degrade your design. To support the DSE the OSCC organises three lectures. This is followed by walk-in sessions, where you can ask questions when you are stuck, or where the OSCC member or TA’s can ask you questions to see whether you are heading in the right direction. The TA’s are also always present during working hours in the Fellowship for questions about PM/SE aspects.

You are kindly requested to keep track of the online schedule to see where and when the (mandatory) PM/SE lectures will be given. The walk-in sessions are typically in the Project Rooms (Spring DSE), or in a dedicated lecture room (Fall DSE).

Some questions or misconceptions have come up regularly in the past. These will be addressed below.

"System Engineering is the task of the System Engineer, and Project Management is the responsibility of the Project Manager."Design is a team effort, and System Engineering will help you do that in the proper way. So, all team members need to "know" System Engineering, apply the tools and techniques, and are also responsible for the outcome! And, if you don't organise your work properly, you will be less efficient and will likely run out of time. Project Management techniques will help you.
"Can we just send the System Engineer to the lectures? He/she will tell us what we need to know."Unfortunately it does not work that way. Since PM/SE is important for the design process and outcome, each team member needs to be aware of all these aspects, not only to apply it but also to support your fellow team members. Designing is all about teamwork!
"Why should we spent so much time on PM/SE? We lose all this time and have nothing left for the actual design!"Realise that PM/SE is designing; the quality of and perhaps even the feasibility of your design depends on it. 
"Deadline approaching! Quickly, who can work on those PM/SE deliverables?"This the wrong approach. These " PM/SE deliverables" are not only deliverables, but they are design tools, which should be used/integrated throughout your design. Therefore, the staff will check whether you actually applied these tools in your design and/or the design process.
"The DSE is far too small to use PM/SE. You can only use it in projects like developing the Ariane 6 launcher."This is a misconception. PM/SE can (and in principle should) be applied to any project. The DSE mimics project design in the aerospace industry, is (very) multi-disciplinary, and represents 10 students * 10 weeks * 40 hrs = 4000 hrs, which, in terms of the BSc program, is a huge project. As the DSE is the last opportunity where you can actually learn how to do PM/SE, it is an essential skill set to take with you when you graduate.

1.  TITLE

The Project Plan


2.  IDENTIFICATION
DID-1
3.  DESCRIPTION
The purpose of the Project Plan is to define the group's overall approach to the technical and management aspects of the design project.  The document provides a complete roadmap of how the group plans to accomplish all the necessary tasks to prepare the design. It represents a commitment by the group for work to be accomplished on the Project. The customer does not want a detailed discussion of a particular design approach, but the approach that shows how the design will be developed through studies, trade-offs, reviews and analysis.

The maximum number of pages is 25 everything included, and is specified by the OSCC.

For large logical diagrams, you are allowed to include A3 pages in you report: 
- 1 for the Work-Flow Diagram
- 2 for the Gantt Chart
These count as 1 page to the page limit. These are specifically to be used for their deliverables, and can not be “traded” to be used elsewhere.


4.  LAST UPDATE
April 7, 2020
5.  CONFIGURATION CONTROL
OSCC (J. Sinke)

7.  PREPARATION INSTRUCTIONS
The Project Plan must show that the group understands the requirements. It has to present a flow of activities that eventually will result in a product that meets these requirements. The plan must explicitly state what services are to be performed at what moments in time and what will be delivered (i.e., briefings, reports, etc.).

Organisational risk assessment
Project Approach with respect to sustainable development (not technical!)

Appendix C: Glossary of DSE terms

In this Appendix definitions are given of all DSE deliverables and the scope of each deliverable for each phase of the project.

DSE work-flow diagrams
The DSE Work Flow Diagram (DSE WFD) shows the logical order of activities to be executed by the project team during the DSE. It contains a number of blocks connected by arrows. The blocks contain a description of activities, hence contain a verb. Activities may be performed in parallel. In exceptional cases also products may be included into the DSE WFD, e.g. reports, reviews, models. Activities specified must be as much as possible specific for the project, i.e. reflect the technical characteristics of the project.
The first version of the DSE WFD covers the period from the Project Plan until the Mid-Term review in detail and the later phase of the DSE in more general terms. The update of the DSE WFD to be presented in the Mid-Term Report must detail this last phase. The pre-MTR WFD can be omitted, unless fundamental changes have occurred in the actual activities executed, of which the knowledge is necessary for a proper understanding of the updated version of the WFD.

DSE work break-down structure
The DSE Work Break-down Structure (DSE WBS) offers another view on the work to be performed and contains the activities as identified in the DSE Work Flow Diagram, ordered in a hierarchical tree (AND tree). The content of each of the block in the DSE WBS is called a Work Package. The WBS may detail the WFD activities to a lower level (e.g. specify that certain activities have a mechanical and an aerodynamic component). It may also contain an indication of the size of the work, the through-put time and the person(s) performing the work.
It follows the same phasing in the reports as the DSE WFD.

DSE Gantt chart
The DSE Gantt Chart (DSE Gantt) presents the Work Packages in the Gantt chart format, i.e. it adds a start and end date to each line item and places them in a time order. It serves initially to find a realistic schedule for the DSE activities and later on to monitor actual progress against the anticipated one. It helps then to define corrective actions, such that the original planned completion dates for products are met. The DSE Gantt should contain milestones and deliverables in addition to activities.
It follows the WFD and WBS cycle, but remains alive for monitoring purposes during the full DSE.

DSE team organization/organogram & HR allocation
The DSE organogram defines the functions of the DSE team members per phase (pre-MTR and MTR till FR) of the DSE. Its companion text defines also the responsibilities of the team members and the decision mechanisms within the DSE team. It is essential that the so-called managerial functions (such as Project Manager, System Engineer, Quality Assurance etc.) are separated from the technical functions (engineering functions in the areas of, for instance, aerodynamics, flight mechanics, stability and control).
WFD, WBS, Gantt chart and organogram together form the elements of the DSE Project Plan. An update of the Project Plan for the final phase of the DSE is to be included in the Mid-Term Report.

Functional flow diagram(s)
The Functional Flow (Block) Diagram (FFBD) shows the logical order of functions the system (product) must perform. It contains a number of blocks describing these functions connected by arrows. Functions are active, hence the description contains a verb. Functions may be performed in parallel (AND junction) or optional functional paths may exist (OR junction). The FFD may also picture the function of the product in its (higher level) environment, specifying, for example its interaction with that environment or detail a (important) part of the product. FFBD’s may be arranged hierarchical, i.e. system functions may be detailed in (lower level) subsystem functions. FFBD’s are presented on a high level and for essential functions in the Baseline Report (BR), are detailed further in the later phases of the project and are included in the Final Report.

Functional breakdown
The Functional Breakdown represents hierarchically the functions that the product or system must perform in the form of a AND tree. Generally it is limited to the system itself and its constituent parts. It presents a different view on the system, and offers for example the opportunity to identify functions that support the other functions identified in the FFBD, e.g. electrical power provision and data handling (typically functions that cannot be placed in “flow form”, so time independent). The Functional Breakdown is presented on a high level and for essential functions in the BR, is detailed further in the later phases of the project and is included in the Final Report. Note that if subsystem functions are essential, they should be included in the breakdown.

Requirements discovery tree
The Requirements Discovery Tree (RDT) is a tool to identify and organize requirements applicable for the product or system to be designed. It is an AND tree hierarchically presenting and detailing the requirements from an operator or user point of view, and is often concentrated on the particular aspects of the system, addressing the “normal” requirements in a summary fashion. The list with user requirements as specified by the customer is not necessarily a complete list, and more user requirements may be added by the students. The user requirements need to be translated into system and mission requirements. If one can foresee that certain sub-system requirements will have an impact on the trade off, these sub-system requirements need to be detailed as much as possible.

The requirements discovery tree is generated early in the project and included in the Baseline Report.

Resource allocation/ Budget breakdown
Technical resources, such as mass, electrical power and computing resources, generally drive the cost of a product and have the tendency to grow during the development. For this reason a maximum value is established at the beginning, including a reserve or contingency, which is carefully managed during the execution of the project. The overall resources are attributed to the components in the system. A first version of these budgets is presented in the Baseline Report, and an update is included in the Final Report, showing sufficient margin for the remaining development after the DSE.
If a technical resource can be identified, which is typical for the product to be designed, e.g. propellant budget for a satellite mission, endurance or range for an aircraft, such a budget shall also be included.

Technical risk assessment/risk map
Technical risk assessment is the activity of identifying and ranking technical risks that may occur in the development of the system or product, with the consequence that technical performance, schedule or cost requirements are not met. To this purpose elements, characteristics, technologies or conditions are identified that may form a risk, the probability that they occur is estimated and the consequence for the success of the mission of the system, if the risk occurs, is assessed. The risks are plotted in a Risk Map, which shows on one axis the probability of occurrence and on the other the consequence. For the highest-ranking risks (high probability and consequence combination) measures in terms of alternative design or additional development activities are identified to decrease the risk (risk mitigation). The effect of risk mitigation should be visualized in a “post-mitigation” Risk Map.
As “old” risks change and disappear and “new” risks appear in the course of the project, the risk assessment is performed in each phase of the project, and a risk map is presented in each report from the Baseline Report onwards.

Design option structuring (tree)
The set of requirements applicable to the system or product to be designed (or to elements of that system) may be met by several conceptual designs. In fact, the process of design is the process of selecting the design option that meets these requirements best. In order to make a reliable choice it is essential to identify all possible solutions and rationally select the best one from the candidates.
The Design Option Tree (DOT) is a tool to support the generation and selection of design options. Each individual subsystem or technical aspect of your system has multiple design options that could lead to a successful system and therefore each should have its own DOT. Contrary to the trees mentioned before, it is an OR tree; each block in the tree is equal to the block above it, and nothing more.
A DOT can be created by a brainstorm with your group, thinking of all possible design options that can be applied to your system. This is the time to be creative, don’t write off options which seem unconventional or undoable immediately, the DOT should be complete. 
The DOT is generated for the Baseline Report. After the requirements are formulated, design options can be assessed using the requirements. Options that turn out to be unfeasible can be eliminated in the Baseline Report already. 
In the Midterm report the remaining options in the DOT will be presented and will be subject to a trade-off. The selection of the best option for each choice will be made.

Contingency Management
Contingency management is the process of establishing and managing the technical resource budgets established for the product or system to be designed. For many of the “traditional” technical resource budgets values have been established with margins that are a function of the phase of the project, the maturity of technology and design, and the particular characteristics of the hardware or software.
The Baseline Report must identify the way the contingencies will be handled in the remaining phases of the project.

Market analysis
The objective of the Market Analysis is to establish the competitive cost and volume of the market for the product or for services the product can provide. This is derived from the current market for comparable products or services and an assessment of the added value of new technology or solutions that could be implemented. Aspects that should be included are the prediction of future markets, the establishing of new markets, and the share you foresee to obtain in these markets. Proper market segmentation may help to address these aspects. In addition, a SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) should be included in the market analysis as well.

A first version of the Market Analysis is produced for the Baseline Report. Its major purpose is to establish a target cost for the system or service, and to generate desired functions and requirements based on the cost and other demands from the market to be competitive. For the Final Report the Market Analysis is updated for the actual characteristics and estimated cost of the product or service.

Interface definition/N2 charts
The design methodology used in the design process is characterized by breaking the system down in more or less functional units or subsystems, which then can be developed in parallel. However, this requires a proper identification and definition of the interfaces (interactions) between the system components and functions. The N2 Chart is a tool, which supports this part of the process. It is a square matrix, where the functions or system components are placed on the diagonal and the interaction between them in the other cells, where outputs are horizontal and inputs vertical. Functions, components and interactions are indicated by a number of words in the cells.
Initially the N2 Chart is used to identify interfaces, to be included in the Mid Term Report. The N2 Chart may also be used to illustrate and document interfaces in the Final Report.
Trade method, rationale and organization
The choice between options for the implementation of a design for the system or for subsystems meeting the requirements is called a trade-off or trade. The method used initially must include all possible solutions in order not to overlook potentially useful and innovative options. Tools used in this phase of the process are brainstorm techniques and the Design Option Tree. This last tool is also used to start narrowing down the set of options to those that are really feasible within the time and resources available.
Once this set of feasible options has been established they need to be objectively compared with each other against a well defined set of criteria. Several methods exist to perform this task: the traditional method of scoring each of the options against (weighted) criteria, the AHP (Analytical Hierarchy Process) method of pair-wise comparison and a graphical comparison method.
It is essential that criteria, weight factors and scoring norms are established and agreed (in the team and with the customer) before the actual trade is executed.
Trades are essentially performed and reported in the Mid Term Report, but may also be needed later on in the design process for more detailed choices.

Trade criteria
Trade Criteria are the criteria against which the different design options are scored or compared. They include generally the (scarce) technical resources available, main performance parameters and more abstract things, as are complexity and safety. It is not useful to include criteria in the trade for which all options score about equal, as such a criterion does not allow for a choice. It is extremely useful to include in the trade the criteria against which one (or more) options score very bad, as they make the difference.
It is also useful to establish before hand for each of the criteria a score below which the option becomes unacceptable (no-go value). Criteria should by preference be quantifiable. If that is not possible, a sharp definition of the qualitative scores (excellent, good, fair, correctable deficiencies, poor, unacceptable) must be provided.

Criteria weight factors
For each of the criteria weight factors must be established and the rationale for the determination of their values must be documented. A reliable method to establish weight factors is by means of the AHP method. Another possibility is to make use of expert opinions to establish their value. The sensitivity of the trade-off to chosen weight criteria should be addressed.

Trade summary table
The result of each trade must be reported in a trade summary table, which documents the options compared, trade criteria, criteria weight factors, the score of each of the options against the criteria and the rationale for the score and the final result.
A convenient format is a table, where the each option is listed as a row, where each column represents a criterion and where the width of the column represents the weight of the criterion. Each box of the table contains a short description of the rationale for the score of the related option for the criterion. The colour of the box represents the score, e.g. red = unacceptable, yellow = correctable deficiencies, green = meets requirement, blue = exceeds requirement. Trade summary tables are part of the Mid Term Report. It may be useful to include the most important trade summary tables also in the Final Report, as they form the rationale behind the final design.

Operations and logistic concept description
The operations and logistic concept description may be given in the form of operational or logistic flow (block) diagrams, illustrating the use or support (e.g. maintenance, ground support) of the system. A first version is produced for the Mid Term report, as the may determine system characteristics that must be part of the design; they may even be part of the concept trade-offs. The operations and logistic concept description is refined and completed for the Final Report.

Project design & development logic
The Project Design & Development (PD&D) logic shows the logical order of activities to be executed in the post-DSE phases of the project. It contains a number of blocks connected by arrows. The blocks contain a description of activities, hence contain a verb. Activities may be performed in parallel. In exceptional cases also products may be included into the D&D logic, e.g. breadboard for critical technologies, tooling, prototypes. Activities specified must be as much as possible specific for the project, i.e. reflect the technical characteristics of the project.
The PD&D logic is included in the Final Report.

Project Gantt chart
The Project Gantt Chart presents the post-DSE activities in the Gantt chart format, i.e. it adds a start and end date to each line item and places them in a time order. It serves as an initial estimate for the schedule for the post-DSE activities.
It is included in the Final Report.

Cost breakdown structure
The Cost Break-down Structure (CBS) contains the cost elements of the post-DSE project activities. It has the shape of an AND tree and serves to identify all elements that contribute to the overall development and production cost of the product or system, for which a preliminary design has been produced during the DSE. It reflects the cost related to the activities defined in the PD&D logic. It is the basis of the cost estimate for the product of system. Typical examples of “standard” CBS’s may be found in literature. The CBS is included in the Final Report.

H/W, S/W block diagrams (interactions, flows)
Hardware and software block diagrams (HWBD and SWBD) serve to illustrate the components of the product or system and their mutual relations and interactions. They may replace large quantities of text, while improving the understanding of the system. They contain blocks connected by lines or arrows; the blocks contain the name of the system component, the lines or arrows may contain a description of the interaction or physical resource flowing though it. HWBDs and SWBDs may be structured hierarchically.
HWBDs and SWBDs may be produced at any moment during the DSE, but shall as a minimum be part of the Final Report.

Electrical block diagram
The Electrical Block Diagram shows the electrical equipment of the product or system and its mutual relations and interactions (power provision, switching lines, power conversions, transmitters, receivers, batteries, generators, etc.).
It is included in the Final Report.

Data handling block diagram
The Data Handling Block Diagram shows the components of the data handling system of the product or system (processors, memories, safeguard or watchdog units). It shows data flows though the system and may specify the contents of the information flowing over the arrows connecting the blocks. It contains data busses, but also discrete command and data lines. In the blocks it specifies characteristics of the hardware represented (e.g. memory size, computing power, data rates).
It is included in the Final Report.

Sustainable development strategy
A sustainable development strategy is presented in the Baseline Report, the Mid Term Report and the Final Report. It addresses both the way sustainability is taken into account in the design and the way the product or system contributes to sustainability.

Requirements Compliance Matrix & Feasibility Analysis
The Requirements Compliance Matrix is a table containing all user requirements and indicates with a tick mark, when a requirement is met. It may specify the actual value that the design meets, but must specify the actual value the design meets, if the design does not meet the requirement. The rationale why the design does not meet the requirement, or which modifications would be required in order to meet the requirement is explained in the accompanying text (Feasibility Analysis). The Requirements Compliance Matrix & Feasibility Analysis are presented in the Final Report.

Sensitivity Analysis
A sensitivity analysis investigates the sensitivity of a design (solution) for a change in major system parameters. The sensitivity analysis can be an effective tool to identify the critical parameters that the design is sensitive to, and to make a convincing case why the design is a robust solution for your mission.

In the Mid Term Report, a sensitivity analysis is used to test the robustness of a trade-off. If design parameters, trade criteria or criteria weights change, it is useful to know what the impact will be on the outcome of the trade-off.
In the Final Report, a sensitivity analysis is used to establish the degree of feasibility of the final design: how does the design react to a change in parameters, and will it still meet the requirements.

Communication flow diagram
The communication flow diagram illustrates the flow of data through the system and to and from its environment. In case the product or system is not controlled by a human operator in direct, physical contact with the product (e.g. a satellite or an UAV), it is mandatory to present such a flow diagram, as it an essential part of the design and often a major cost factor. It may play a role in the design option selection, but certainly forms part of the final design. In the former case it is part of de design option selection presented in the Mid Term Report, in the latter it is part of the Final Report.
The Communication Flow (Block) Diagram contains all elements that are part of the communication chain in blocks and represents data and command flows as arrows, where the content of the flow is annotated as text with those arrows. It may also include notes indication the data volume and data rate of elements and flows.

Validation & Verification Procedures
The Validation & Verification Procedures enter the project in two ways. In the first place all used numerical and/or computer models need to be verified (according to the definitions and procedures covered in the course AE3205 – Simulation, Verification and Validation) before these models can actually be used during both the conceptual and detailed design. The verification procedures and results need to be included in both the Midterm and the Final report.

In the second place, the Validation & Verification Procedures describe how the proof will be obtained that the product or system meets the requirements imposed on it. It distinguishes between test, analysis, simulation, review of design (documentation) and similarity (i.e. a comparable design has been developed and operated successfully) as methods to obtain that proof..

Manufacturing, Assembly, Integration plan (production plan)
The Manufacturing, Assembly & Integration Plan (MAI Plan) gives a time ordered outline of the activities required to construct the product from its constituent parts. It may include parallel activities. The core of the MAI Plan is a flow diagram illustrating this outline. Short texts may elaborate on it. A first version of the Plan is presented in the Mid-Term Report; a more elaborated final version in the Final Report.
The MAI Plan is mandatory in case series production of the product is part of the assignment.

Return on investment
In order to establish the Return of Investment (RoI) the following data related to the product shall be known or established:
Market price of the product (class),
Market volume for the product (class),
Achievable market share for the product,
Development cost of the product,
Production cost of the product.
Direct operational cost
A positive balance between number of products sold and the total cost divided by the total cost is the RoI. The RoI is established at the end of the project after the cost estimate, and is presented in the Final Report.

Reliability, Availability, Maintainability, and Safety (RAMS) characteristics
A first version of the RAMS characteristics of the product or system is established in the Mid-Term Report. This first version is generally based on similarity with existing products. For the Final Report the RAMS characteristics are refined, reflecting the actual design. They shall at least address a list of safety critical functions, the redundancy philosophy applied, the expected reliability and availability, and, for maintainable systems, an outline of scheduled and non-scheduled maintenance activities.

Performance analysis
Performance analysis addresses the quantified performance of the primary functions of the design, and is a major means to show compliance of the design with the requirements imposed on it. Typical analyses are flight profile diagrams, payload-range diagrams, climb performance, noise characteristics, emissions, pointing accuracy, coverage, visiting frequency, mission duration, mass and power need, etc. Standard, discipline specific analysis techniques are used. A first estimate is produced as part of the concept selection process and presented in the Mid Term Report; the final and complete version is included in the Final Report.

Configuration / layout (internal /external)
The configuration / layout shows the physical appearance of the product or system. It is mainly a set of sketches and drawings illustrating the internal and external layout with a short textual explanation. The layout contains also a block diagram identifying the electronic units carried on-board.
A first version is presented in the Mid Term Report, generally for all design options involved in the concept selection, concentrating on those aspects that are essential to provide the trade-off. The final, more detailed, version of the final design is included in the Final Report.

Spacecraft system characteristics
Spacecraft system characteristics include as a minimum the mass, power and propellant budget, command and telemetry data rates, communications link budget, memory size, pointing accuracy, thrust, coverage, visiting frequency, mission duration and profile, ground station selection, launcher selection, decommissioning and de-orbiting, plus the astrodynamic characteristics described below.
A first, limited version of design driving characteristics is included in the Mid-Term Report, a final, extended version including all relevant characteristics is given in the Final Report.

Aircraft system characteristics
Aircraft system characteristics include e.g. fuel and hydraulic system layout, auxiliary power estimate, environmental control, etc.
A first, limited version of design driving characteristics is included in the Mid-Term Report, a final, extended version including all relevant characteristics is given in the Final Report.

Aerodynamic characteristics estimate
Aerodynamic characteristics include e.g. lift, drag, aerodynamic moments, drag polar, etc., as well as the selected wing profile (the airfoil), if applicable. A first estimate of design driving characteristics is included in the Mid-Term Report, a final, extended version including all relevant characteristics is given in the Final Report.

Structural characteristics
Structural characteristics include e.g. loading diagrams, stresses, bending, flutter, lowest natural frequencies, design loads, etc.
A first version of design driving characteristics is included in the Mid-Term Report, a final, extended version including all relevant characteristics is given in the Final Report.

Stability and control characteristics
Stability and control characteristics include e.g. control forces, CoM limits, attitude control torques, momentum storage and unload capacity, disturbance torques, control frequency, etc.
A first version of design driving characteristics is included in the Mid-Term Report, a final, extended version including all relevant characteristics is given in the Final Report.

Material characteristics
The material characteristics include e.g. yield, ultimate, fatigue, etc., and should be specified for all applicable materials. In addition, the location of the applicable materials for system/sub-systems should be specified. A first version of design driving characteristics is included in the Mid-Term Report, a final, extended version including all relevant characteristics is given in the Final Report.

Astrodynamic characteristics
Astrodynamic characteristics include the orbit, trajectory, decay rate, ∆V-budget, coverage and visibility, mission duration, etc. Generally they play a large role in the establishment of the requirements, the design option selection and the final mission design.
A first version, as far as needed for the sizing of the system, is presented in the Baseline Report. The Mid-Term Report presents for all options and the selected design the characteristic properties, while the Final Report contains the final, detailed description of the astrodynamic characteristics.

Always include the citation date for online references. Break Web site addresses after punctuation, and do not hyphenate at line breaks.
Computer Software
[16] TAPP, Thermochemical and Physical Properties, Software Package, Ver. 1.0, E. S. Microware, Hamilton, OH, 1992.
Include a version number and the company name and location of software packages.
Patents
Patents appear infrequently. Be sure to include the patent number and date.
[17] Scherrer, R., Overholster, D., and Watson, K., Lockheed Corp., Burbank, CA, U.S. Patent Application for a “Vehicle,” Docket No. P-01-1532, filed 11 Feb. 1979.
Private Communications and Web Sites
References to private communications and personal Web site addresses are not permitted. They may, however, be incorporated into the main text of a manuscript or may appear in footnotes.
Unpublished Papers and Books
Unpublished works can be used as references as long as they are being considered for publication or can be located by the reader (such as papers that are part of an archival collection). If a journal paper or a book is being considered for publication, choose the format that reflects the status of the work (depending upon whether it has been accepted for publication):
[18] Doe, J., “Title of Paper,” Name of Journal (to be published).
[19] Doe, J., “Title of Chapter,” Name of Book, edited by…, Publisher’s name and location (to be published).
[20] Doe, J., “Title of Work,” Name of Archive, Univ. (or organization), City, State, Year (unpublished).
Unpublished works in an archive must include the name of the archive and the name and location of the university or other organization where the archive is held. Also include any cataloguing information that may be provided.
